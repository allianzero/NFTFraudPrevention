# NFTFraudPrevention

A guide to NFT fraud: fake collections, phishing mints, wash trading, stolen art, and malicious approvals — and how to verify, buy, and hold NFTs without becoming a victim. NFTs are uniquely fraud-prone because their value depends on authenticity, provenance, and hype, all of which are easy to fake and hard for beginners to verify.

> This guide is educational only, not financial or legal advice. Owning an NFT usually does not mean owning the copyright to its art — see NFTBasicsGuide for what ownership actually means.

## 1. Why NFTs attract fraud

- **Authenticity is the product.** An NFT's value depends on it being the "real" one from a real project. A perfect copy with a different contract address looks identical to a beginner.
- **Hype moves fast.** Launches, mints, and drops create urgency, and urgency overrides verification.
- **Approvals are powerful.** Minting and trading require wallet approvals that, if malicious, can drain NFTs and tokens alike.
- **Anonymity is normal.** Anonymous teams, pseudonymous artists, and faceless marketplaces are common, which removes accountability when fraud happens.
- **Illiquidity hides fraud.** A fake collection can show a high "floor price" with no real buyers, because listings are not sales.

## 2. Fake collections: the number one trap

A fake collection copies a popular project's name, art, description, and branding, but deploys a different contract.

**How to verify a collection is real — every time, before buying:**

1. **Contract address, from official sources only.** Get the address from the project's official website or verified social accounts, and compare it exactly — character by character — with the marketplace listing. Name, art, and description can be copied perfectly; the contract address cannot be faked without being a different contract.
2. **Official links, not search results.** Follow links from the project's own site. Search results and ads for popular collections are frequently faked.
3. **Creation date and history.** A real, established collection usually has months of trading history across many distinct wallets. A collection created yesterday with sudden volume is suspicious.
4. **Holder distribution.** If a handful of wallets hold most of the supply, prices can be manipulated easily, and "sales" may be wash trading (see section 5).
5. **Creator and team verification.** Is the creator's wallet or social account verifiably linked to the project through its official channels? A marketplace "verified" badge alone is not sufficient — badges have been faked and misapplied.

**If any check fails, do not buy.** There will always be another collection. A fake NFT is worthless the moment you realise it is fake, and refunds do not exist.

## 3. Phishing mints and malicious sites

A phishing mint site looks like a project's official mint page, but its "Mint" button triggers a malicious transaction: draining your wallet, approving unlimited transfers, or sending your NFTs to the attacker.

**Defences:**

- Only mint from links on the project's official site, bookmarked by you. Never mint from a link in a DM, post, ad, or email.
- **Read the transaction in your wallet before signing.** What function is being called? What token or NFT is being approved or transferred, and to whom? If you do not understand it, do not sign it.
- **Use a separate minting wallet** containing only what you need for that mint. Never mint from a wallet holding valuable NFTs or large token balances.
- **Beware of urgency tricks:** "mint ends in 10 minutes", "only 3 left", "whitelist closing now". Real projects may have real deadlines, but scammers manufacture urgency to prevent verification. A deadline that prevents you from verifying is itself a warning sign.
- **Check the mint price and gas.** A "free mint" with enormous gas, or a mint price far above the announced price, suggests a fake or manipulated page.

## 4. Stolen art and unauthorised mints

Anyone can mint an NFT of art they did not create. "On the blockchain" does not mean "authorised by the artist".

- **Stolen art mints:** an artist's work is copied and minted by someone else, who sells it as if it were legitimate. Buyers get a token pointing to stolen art, with no rights and no recourse when marketplaces delist it.
- **Unauthorised derivatives:** collections that copy another project's art with minor changes, without permission, trading on the original's reputation.
- **AI-generated fakes:** art generated to mimic a known artist's style, marketed as that artist's work.

**Defences:** verify the creator through official channels (the artist's own site and social accounts, linked to the collection). Check whether the artist has publicly claimed the collection. If the artist has publicly disowned it, believe the artist, not the seller.

## 5. Wash trading and fake volume

**Wash trading** is when the same person or group trades an NFT back and forth between wallets they control, creating fake volume and fake price history.

Signs of wash trading:

- The same few wallets appear repeatedly as buyer and seller.
- Volume is high, but the number of distinct buyers is tiny.
- Prices rise in neat, repeated steps with no organic variation.
- Sales happen at implausible prices (far above or below the floor) with no external reason.
- A collection's volume spikes suddenly, then collapses, with no news or product change.

**Why it matters:** wash trading makes a collection look popular and valuable, attracting real buyers who become the exit liquidity when wash traders stop. A high floor price with no real buyers is not a valuation — it is a listing.

**Defence:** check distinct buyer counts, holder distribution, and sale history over time, not just volume and floor price. Tools on major marketplaces and analytics sites show these; use them before buying.

## 6. Pump-and-dump and influencer fraud

A typical pattern:

1. Insiders or influencers accumulate a collection quietly.
2. They hype it aggressively: "next big thing", "100× incoming", "celebrity X just bought in" (often false or paid).
3. New buyers push prices up.
4. Insiders sell into the hype and disappear. Prices collapse.

**Defences:**

- Ignore price predictions from anyone selling or holding the collection, unless they disclose their position and reasoning transparently.
- Distrust "celebrity bought" claims unless verified from the celebrity's own channels.
- If a collection's entire case is "influencers are talking about it", it has no case.
- Never buy based on a DM tip, a private group "alpha" leak, or a countdown timer.

## 7. Malicious approvals and wallet draining

NFT fraud is not only about buying fakes — it is about losing real NFTs through approvals.

- **Unlimited marketplace approvals:** approving a marketplace to transfer "all" your NFTs is convenient, but if that marketplace is compromised or malicious, it can transfer everything.
- **Fake "claim" or "upgrade" sites:** a site asks you to connect and "claim", "stake", "upgrade", or "verify" your NFT. The transaction it asks you to sign actually transfers your NFT to the attacker.
- **Airdropped NFTs:** an unknown NFT appears in your wallet. Its metadata links to a site asking you to connect and claim a reward. Connecting is the trap.

**Defences:**

- Use a separate wallet for minting, claiming, and experimenting. Keep valuable NFTs in a vault wallet that rarely connects to anything.
- Read every approval: what is being approved, to whom, and is it limited or unlimited?
- Revoke approvals regularly, especially after using a new site.
- Never send your NFT to anyone for "verification", "upgrading", "repair", or "staking" outside a protocol you have thoroughly verified. There is no legitimate process that requires you to send an NFT to a stranger.
- Unknown NFT in your wallet? Hide it or ignore it. Do not list it, do not visit its site, do not interact with it.

## 8. Marketplace and listing fraud

- **Fake listings and bids:** a listing that looks like a bargain but leads to a phishing site, or a bid that disappears when you try to accept it.
- **Listing price manipulation:** a seller lists an NFT, then uses wash trades to create a fake sale history justifying a high price.
- **Royalty and fee deception:** a marketplace or seller misstates royalties, fees, or the collection's terms. Read the marketplace's fee schedule and the collection's royalty terms before trading.
- **Delisting risk:** marketplaces can delist collections (for fraud, copyright claims, or policy reasons), making them hard to sell even if you own a legitimate token. Delisting does not delete your token, but it can destroy its market.

**Defence:** trade on reputable marketplaces, read fee and royalty terms, and verify every listing's contract address — not just its image and name.

## 9. If you bought a fake or were scammed

1. **Stop interacting with the scammer's site or contracts.** Do not "claim", "verify", or pay fees to recover anything.
2. **Move unaffected assets** to a fresh wallet if your wallet or approvals may be compromised.
3. **Revoke approvals** for suspicious contracts.
4. **Preserve evidence:** transaction hashes, contract addresses, URLs, screenshots, chat logs, and dates.
5. **Report:** to the marketplace (request delisting of the fake collection), to the impersonated project (so they can warn others), and to your local cybercrime / fraud reporting service.
6. **Accept the likely outcome:** refunds for fake NFT purchases are rare. Marketplaces may delist fakes, but they generally do not refund buyers. Focus on reporting and on preventing the next loss.
7. **Do not pay recovery services.** Anyone charging a fee to recover your NFT or funds is almost certainly running a second scam.

## 10. Safe NFT checklist

- [ ] Contract address verified from official sources, exactly.
- [ ] Creator verified through official channels.
- [ ] Collection history, holder distribution, and distinct buyers checked.
- [ ] Mint or purchase only from official links, bookmarked by me.
- [ ] Transaction and approval read in my wallet before signing.
- [ ] Minting wallet separate from vault wallet holding valuable NFTs.
- [ ] Price, fees, gas, and royalties understood before confirming.
- [ ] No urgency, secrecy, or "exclusive" pressure driving the decision.
- [ ] I can explain, in one sentence, why this NFT might hold value — beyond "someone else will pay more".

If most boxes are unchecked, do not buy yet. The NFT market rewards patience and verification far more than speed.
